📖1. Introduction to Databases▼

Lecture 1, Ch 1–2. Lowest-stakes topic — likely 1 short question, not a full analytical question.

DBMS vs. File System LOW

A DBMS solves the redundancy, inconsistency, and lack of concurrency-control problems of flat file systems by centralizing data with a shared schema, integrity constraints, and controlled access.

Three-Schema Architecture LOW

External (user views) → Conceptual (structure/constraints for the whole DB) → Internal (physical storage structures, access paths/indexes).

Data Independence LOW

Ability to change a schema at one level without impacting the level above it. Logical: change the conceptual schema without changing external schemas/apps (e.g. add a column not shown in the app). Physical: change the internal schema without changing the conceptual schema (e.g. reorganize file structure or add an index).

Schema vs. State LOW

Schema = the structure/description of a database (should rarely change). State (a.k.a. instance/occurrence/snapshot) = the actual data at a moment in time (changes on every update). DDL changes the schema; DML changes the state.

📖2. ER Model▼

Lecture 2, Ch 3.

Entity Sets & Attribute Types HIGH

Entity = a distinguishable real-world object. Attribute types: simple (atomic), composite (has sub-parts, e.g. Address→City/Street), multi-valued (double oval, e.g. Phone), derived (dotted oval, e.g. Age from DOB — not stored). Key attribute: underlined, unique per entity, must not be multivalued — an entity can have more than one key attribute.

Relationship Sets & Cardinality HIGH

Cardinality ratios: 1:1, 1:N, M:N — written on the connecting lines. Participation constraint: total/mandatory (double line, every entity instance must participate) vs. partial/optional (single line). Degree = number of participating entity types: 2 = binary, 3 = ternary, n = n-ary. A relationship needing its own key (weak entity's owner link) is an identifying relationship — shown as a double diamond.

ExWorked example: XYZ Company employee/dept/project ER diagramHIGH▼
1
EMPLOYEE (Ssn, Name[Fname,Minit,Lname], Bdate, Address, Sex, Salary) — regular entity, Ssn is key.
2
DEPARTMENT (Name, Number, Locations[multivalued]) — WORKS_FOR (N:1 Employee→Dept), MANAGES (1:1), CONTROLS (1:N Dept→Project).
3
PROJECT (Name, Number, Location) — WORKS_ON is M:N between Employee and Project, with relationship attribute Hours.
4
SUPERVISION is a recursive 1:N relationship on Employee (Supervisor/Supervisee roles). DEPENDENT is a weak entity (double rectangle) identified via DEPENDENTS_OF, partial key = Name.
Key insight: This exact diagram reappears in the Schema Mapping lecture as the canonical worked example for ER→relational conversion — know it in both directions.
📖3. EER Model▼

Lecture 3, Ch 4. Extends ER with subclass/superclass, inheritance, specialization/generalization.

Specialization vs. Generalization HIGH
  • Specialization (top-down): defining a set of subclasses of a superclass, based on some distinguishing characteristic of its entities (e.g. Student → {Undergrad, Graduate} by degree level).
  • Generalization (bottom-up): the reverse — defining a superclass from several existing entity types that share common attributes (e.g. TheoryRoom + LabRoom → Classroom, with shared attributes like RoomNo/capacity/boardType moved up and only local attributes like equipmentList/type left in the subclasses).
  • A superclass can have multiple independent specializations at once — e.g. Faculty can be specialized simultaneously by contract type {Contractual, Full-time}, by designation {Lecturer, Assistant Prof, Associate Prof, Prof}, and by department-head status {DeptHead}.
  • A subclass can be shared by more than one superclass (a lattice, not just a tree) — e.g. StudentTutor can be a subclass of both Student and Employee at once. Subclass/superclass is also called an IS-A relationship (an Undergrad IS-A Student).
Inheritance & Local Attributes HIGH

A subclass entity inherits all attributes and relationships of its superclass (and, transitively, of every ancestor superclass up the hierarchy), plus it may define its own local attributes/relationships. If an entity is a member of any subclass, it must also belong to the superclass — but a superclass member need not belong to any subclass (unless the specialization is total).

Disjoint/Overlapping & Total/Partial HIGH
  • Disjoint (d): an entity can belong to at most one subclass of that specialization. Overlapping (o): an entity can belong to more than one subclass of the same specialization simultaneously.
  • Total (double line from superclass to circle): every superclass member must belong to at least one subclass. Partial (single line): membership is optional. 4 combinations exist: disjoint-total, disjoint-partial, overlapping-total, overlapping-partial.
Common error: Confusing disjoint/overlapping (can an entity be in >1 subclass?) with total/partial (must every entity be in some subclass?) — these are independent, orthogonal constraints.
Weak Entities (recap from ER) MED

No key of its own — identified only via a foreign key to its owner entity plus a partial key. Shown as a double-outlined rectangle with a double-outlined diamond identifying relationship.

ExWorked example (from the lecture's own practice slide): University Person hierarchyHIGH▼
1
PERSON (Ssn, Name, Sex, Address, Birth_date) is the superclass. "There are only 3 types of persons: Employee, Alumnus, Student" and "a person can be of different types at the same time" → specialization is total (every person is one of the 3) and overlapping (o) (can be more than one, e.g. an employee who is also an alumnus).
2
EMPLOYEE adds Salary. "Employees must belong to only 1 type: Staff, Faculty, Student Assistant" → disjoint (d), total.
3
STUDENT adds Major. "Students can be in any one of only two types: undergraduate or graduate" → disjoint (d), total — GRADUATE_STUDENT adds Degree_program, UNDERGRADUATE_STUDENT adds Class.
4

5
ALUMNUS has a composite-multivalued attribute Degrees(Year, Degree, Major) — one alumnus can hold several degrees.
Key insight: This single diagram demonstrates almost every EER concept at once — overlapping + disjoint in the same hierarchy, a shared/lattice subclass, and a composite-multivalued attribute. If one comprehensive EER question appears, this is the shape it's likely to take.
📖4. Relational Model & Constraints▼

Lecture 4, Ch 5.

Relation, Schema, State HIGH

Relation = table. Schema = R(A1,...,An) — the structure. State = the actual data at a point in time. Tuples are unordered; attributes are ordered (reordering columns changes the schema).

Superkey → Candidate Key → Primary Key HIGH
  • Superkey: any attribute set with unique values per tuple.
  • Key: a minimal superkey — removing any attribute breaks uniqueness.
  • All keys of a relation are candidate keys; exactly one is chosen as the primary key (convention: smallest value set, e.g. ID over Email).
  • Foreign key: references a primary key of another (or the same, for recursive) relation.
Four Relational Integrity Constraints HIGH
  • Domain constraint: every value must be from its attribute's declared domain (type/range), or NULL if allowed.
  • Key constraint: every key value must be unique across tuples.
  • Entity integrity constraint: no primary key attribute (or any component if composite) may be NULL.
  • Referential integrity constraint: a foreign key value must either match an existing primary key value in the referenced table, or be NULL (only if the FK isn't also part of this table's own primary key).
Exam note: INSERT can violate all 4 constraints. DELETE can only violate referential integrity (of rows referencing the deleted key). UPDATE's violation risk depends on which attribute is modified — same rules as insert/delete combined.
ON DELETE / ON UPDATE Actions MED

Restrict (block the operation), Cascade (propagate the delete/update to referencing rows), Set Null (only valid if the FK is not part of the referencing table's own primary key).

ExSQL DDL enforcing all constraints (worked example)MED▼
CREATE TABLE Student (
  ID INT,
  Name VARCHAR(100) NOT NULL,
  EmailAdd VARCHAR(100) UNIQUE,
  CGPA DECIMAL(3,2),
  Dept CHAR(3),
  PRIMARY KEY (ID),
  FOREIGN KEY (Dept) REFERENCES Department(DeptID),
  CHECK (CGPA >= 0.00 AND CGPA <= 4.00)
);
Key insight: PRIMARY KEY enforces key + entity integrity together. UNIQUE enforces key constraint on non-PK candidate keys. FOREIGN KEY...REFERENCES enforces referential integrity. CHECK/data type enforces domain.
🧮5. ER/EER → Relational Schema Mapping▼

Lecture 5. This is the algorithmic core of the midterm — expect a full scenario-to-schema question.

Steps 1–7 (ER → Relational) HIGH
  • Step 1 — Regular entities: one table per entity; simple attributes + composite sub-parts become columns; multivalued deferred to Step 6; one candidate key underlined as PK.
  • Step 2 — Weak entities: owner's PK added as FK; combined with weak entity's partial key = weak entity's PK.
  • Step 3 — Binary N:M: separate relationship table; both entities' PKs added as FKs, together forming the PK (cross-referencing approach).
  • Step 4 — Binary 1:N: foreign-key approach — PK of the "1" side entity added as FK into the "N" side table (space-efficient; cross-referencing works but wastes space).
  • Step 5 — Binary 1:1: 3 options — cross-referencing, merged relation, or foreign-key (put FK on the side with total participation to avoid NULLs).
  • Step 6 — Multivalued attributes: separate table; owner's PK as FK + the attribute value, both underlined as combined PK.
  • Step 7 — N-ary relationships: separate table; all participating entities' PKs added as FKs forming the combined PK.
Step 8 — EER Specialization/Generalization (4 options) HIGH
  • 8A — Multiple tables (superclass + each subclass): always valid for any disjoint/overlapping, total/partial combination. Superclass PK becomes FK = PK in each subclass table.
  • 8B — Subclass tables only (no superclass table): only valid if specialization is total — otherwise members not in any subclass are lost.
  • 8C — Single table, one type attribute: only valid if disjoint — one discriminator column (e.g. StudentType) suffices only when membership is mutually exclusive.
  • 8D — Single table, N flag attributes: works for any constraint combination but wastes space for disjoint cases (N boolean flag columns instead of 1).
Common error: Picking 8B or 8C without checking the total/partial or disjoint/overlapping constraint first — these options are conditionally valid, not universally applicable like 8A/8D.
ExWorked example: EMPLOYEE/DEPARTMENT/PROJECT ER → relational tables (cross-check with Step 1–7)HIGH▼

Pattern: Dept_Locations = multivalued attribute (Step 6). Works_On = M:N relationship (Step 3). Project→Department uses Step 4 (1:N, FK on the N side). Dependent = weak entity (Step 2).
📖6. Normalization▼

Lecture 6. Flagged as the historically hardest-scoring topic — allocate extra practice time. Scope note (confirmed by full slide review): the deck teaches 1NF, 2NF, and 3NF in full, each with a definition and worked example. BCNF is named only once, on the "Normal Forms" overview slide ("normal forms are 1NF, 2NF, 3NF, BCNF, 4NF, 5NF... typically normalized up to 3NF and sometimes BCNF") — no BCNF definition or example is given anywhere in the deck. Treat 1NF/2NF/3NF as the confirmed depth for this exam.

Why Normalize — the Three Anomalies HIGH
  • Insertion anomaly: can't add a fact without an unrelated fact also being present.
  • Deletion anomaly: deleting one fact accidentally destroys another, unrelated fact.
  • Modification (update) anomaly: a fact stored redundantly across rows must be updated in every row, or the data becomes inconsistent.
Functional Dependencies (FDs) HIGH

X → Y means: for any two tuples, if they agree on X, they must agree on Y. FDs are the formal basis for every normal form check — always identify the primary key and all FDs first.

1NF / 2NF / 3NF Definitions HIGH
  • 1NF: no multi-valued or composite attributes in any cell; no nested tables.
  • 2NF: 1NF + no partial dependency — no non-prime attribute depends on only part of a composite primary key.
  • 3NF: 2NF + no transitive dependency — no non-prime attribute depends on another non-prime attribute (only on the whole key).
ExWorked example: Student(StID, CourseCode, Semester, StudentName, AttemptedCredits, CourseTitle, Grade, ProjID, ProjectMarks, ProjectName) → normalize to 3NFHIGH▼
1
FDs given: StID→StudentName; CourseCode→CourseTitle; StID,Semester→AttemptedCredits; ProjID→ProjectName,ProjectMarks. PK = {StID, CourseCode, Semester}.
2
1NF: already satisfied (no multivalued/composite cells, no nested tables).
3
Not 2NF: partial dependencies exist — StudentName depends on only StID (not full key), CourseTitle on only CourseCode, AttemptedCredits on only {StID,Semester}. Decompose into: Student(StID,StudentName), Course(CourseCode,CourseTitle), Student_Credits(StID,Semester,AttemptedCredits), Student_Grades(StID,CourseCode,Semester,Grade,ProjID,ProjectMarks,ProjectName).
4
Still not 3NF: in Student_Grades, ProjectName/ProjectMarks depend transitively on ProjID (a non-prime attribute), not on the full key. Decompose further: Student_Grades(StID,CourseCode,Semester,Grade,ProjID) + Student_Projects(ProjID,ProjectMarks,ProjectName).
5
Final 3NF schema: 5 tables — Student, Course, Student_Credits, Student_Grades, Student_Projects.
Exam note: The practice sheet variant of this question explicitly instructs "show the complete current schema after EACH normalization step" — losing marks for skipping intermediate schemas is a real risk; don't jump straight to the final answer.
🧮7. Relational Algebra▼

Lecture 7, Ch 8. Zero partial-credit risk per classmate report — grading is strict from the first operator. Build expressions incrementally and verify each step.

Unary Operations (1 relation → 1 relation) HIGH
  • SELECT σC(R) — filters tuples by condition C (=, <, ≤, >, ≥, ≠, combined with AND/OR/NOT).
  • PROJECT πA1,...,An(R) — returns specified columns, duplicates removed (set semantics).
  • RENAME ρR'(B1,...,Bn)(R) — renames the relation and/or its attributes; changes schema, not instance.
Binary Operations (2 relations → 1 relation) HIGH
  • UNION R∪S, INTERSECTION R∩S, DIFFERENCE R−S — require R and S to be union-compatible: same number of attributes, same order, compatible domains.
  • CARTESIAN PRODUCT R×S — combines every tuple of R with every tuple of S; R×S ≠ S×R; rarely meaningful alone.
  • JOIN R⋈CS — equivalent to σC(R×S); merges on a condition over common/related attributes.
Why it matters: SQL's SELECT/FROM/WHERE/GROUP BY/HAVING/ORDER BY compiles internally into an RA tree ("logical plan") before execution — this is literally how a real RDBMS query optimizer works.
Query Trees MED

Internal representation of an RA expression: leaf nodes = base tables, internal nodes = operations (σ, π, ρ, ⋈), root = final result. Draw bottom-up: base tables first, then apply operations upward matching the expression's nesting.

ExWorked example set — schema: Student(SID,SName,address,dept,totalCredits), ST(SID,assignedCourse), Faculty(FIN,FName,LName,Desk#), Course(CCode,CName), FacultyCourses(FIN,CCode,Section)HIGH▼
1
Students with ≥70 credits from 'CS' dept: σtotalCredits≥70 AND dept='CS'(Student)
2
FName and Desk# of all faculty: πFName,Desk#(Faculty)
3
SID, SName of Students from 'CSE' dept: πSID,SName(σdept='CSE'(Student))
4
Rename Course's CCode,CName to Code,CTitle: ρCode,CTitle(Course)
5
ST's SName, dept, assignedCourse (needs a join first): πSName,dept,assignedCourse(ST ⋈SID=SID Student)
6
Faculty FName/Desk# teaching CSE370 or CSE230: πFName,Desk#(σCCode='CSE370' OR CCode='CSE230'(Faculty ⋈FIN=FIN FacultyCourses))
7
Faculty FName/Desk# teaching 'Database Systems' or 'AI' (needs course name, so join Course too — use assignment operator to decompose): R ← (Faculty ⋈FIN=FIN FacultyCourses) ⋈CCode=CCode Course;  πFName,Desk#(σCCode='Database Systems' OR CCode='AI'(R))
Pattern: When a query needs a column that lives on a different table than the filter condition, join first, then project/select. Use the assignment operator (←) to break a 3+ table query into readable intermediate steps — this also reduces the blast radius of a single mistake under the strict grading rule.

🧠Mnemonics & Memory Aids▼
1NF → 2NF → 3NF ladder
1NF kills repeating groups. 2NF kills partial dependency (needs a composite key to even apply). 3NF kills transitive dependency (non-key depending on non-key).
σπρ∪∩−×⋈
Unary ops first (σ select, π project, ρ rename) — alphabetically-ish Greek. Binary ops need union-compatibility (∪∩−) except × and ⋈ which don't.
"1 side gets the FK on the N side"
For 1:N mapping — the foreign key always lives on the table connected to the "N" (many) end, pointing back to the "1" end's primary key.
Total participation → put the FK there
For 1:1 mapping via foreign-key approach — placing the FK on the entity with total participation avoids unnecessary NULLs.
