## Reason Patterns

*Recurring patterns across the semester, summarizing strengths and failure modes.*

- **Late start / deadline-day execution:** Lab Assignment 1 was completed and submitted on the deadline day, 5 minutes late. Lab Prep was missed entirely. CSE321 Quiz 1 had insufficient preparation. Root cause consistent across all three: tasks are not started until the deadline is imminent. **Escalated (CSE331 Assignment 1 Part A):** same pattern, but with an external commitment (competition) on the actual deadline day — leaving it to the deadline day meant that one unexpected event caused a total miss rather than a late submission. A two-day extension also failed to convert to a submission due to post-deadline procrastination. **New variant (CSE331 Assignment 2 Part B, #41):** the deadline-day squeeze itself was survived — submitted with ~2 minutes to spare, genuinely correct, not rushed-and-wrong — but the time pressure produced a new failure mode: reaching for an excuse to justify skipping the work rather than doing it under pressure. Self-corrected on direct questioning. Worth naming explicitly: under time pressure, the instinct can shift to looking for an off-ramp rather than pushing through.
- **Skipping practice materials:** CSE370 Quiz 1 prep was theory-only; practice sheets were not used. CSE331 Quiz 1 covered theory but skipped DFA construction practice. CSE321 Quiz 1 had insufficient revision overall. Pattern is consistent: conceptual understanding is pursued but mechanical practice and revision are skipped.
- **No buffer time built in:** A 5-minute miss on a submission is not a content failure — it is a time management failure. Submissions need to be ready at least 30 minutes before the deadline. **Recurred and worsened:** CSE370 Lab Assignment 2 was finished at literally the 11:59 PM cutoff, and the Google Form closed before submission could complete. This is a two-time pattern specific to CSE370 lab deliverables — start earlier in the day, not the evening of the deadline. **Partial break in the pattern:** Lab Assignment 3 was submitted ~33 minutes before the deadline, the first CSE370 lab deliverable with any real buffer — though work still started the evening of the deadline, so the buffer came from working fast, not from starting early.
- **Understanding gaps surfacing only at verification, not self-review:** Lab Assignment 3's Q7 (top-5 highest loan amounts) had a logic bug — sorted ascending before `LIMIT 5`, which silently selected the 5 lowest amounts instead of the top 5. The query ran without error and returned a plausible-looking table, so it would have gone unnoticed without an external check against the actual question text.
- **Theory without application:** Several quizzes show the same gap — concepts understood at a surface level, application not drilled. For procedural topics (DFA construction, `fork()` tracing, IPC scenario reasoning), theory recall is necessary but not sufficient. **Extended to lab quizzes:** CSE370 Lab Quiz 1 fits the same pattern — a purpose-built revision guide with query-writing practice existed beforehand, and the gap wasn't missing material, it was not working through the practice problems. **Recurred, worse outcome:** CSE370 Lab Quiz 2 — same self-reported cause, but only 2 of 5 questions attempted, neither accurate. Two lab quizzes in a row with the identical stated cause is a specific, repeating pattern for this course's lab-quiz format, not a general revision problem.
- **Compressed-timeline completion without an audit pass:** CSE370 Lab Project prep and the paired group session were both completed by the hard deadline, but self-reported as "not polished enough" — the cause named directly as a compressed timeline. Finishing all required pieces left no slack for the verification/audit step. Worth flagging early on future multi-piece deliverables: budget explicit time for an audit pass, not just for finishing each piece once.
- **Underprepared but recovered on fundamentals:** CSE321 Lab Assessment 1 fits the "insufficient preparation" pattern, but with a wrinkle — a prior-cohort friend and the faculty had both signaled the assessment would be simpler than the broad stated syllabus implied, and that signal wasn't taken seriously going in. The outcome held up mainly because of retained HSC-era C fundamentals, not this cycle's readiness — the margin was thin and covered by pre-existing skill.
- **First successful live group defense (positive signal):** the Lab Project topic approval required defending two proposed ideas in a viva, with faculty selecting one on the spot. The preferred option was approved. A different skill from solo exam-prep — the first recorded instance this semester of successfully pitching/defending original work live.
- **First proactive, iterative project workflow (positive signal):** both Lab Project milestones due the same day were built well ahead of the deadline through iterative design, and — critically — a deliberate cross-check of the two deliverables against each other before submission, which caught two real inconsistencies that would otherwise have gone in unnoticed. Breaks cleanly from the dominant late-start pattern seen everywhere else this semester.
- **Solid on mechanism, loses reserved/edge details under live questioning:** the CSE321 Term Project Viva tested real understanding of the inode bitmap; the miss was forgetting that one inode is permanently reserved for the root directory, so the actual answer is one less than the raw constant. A structural/edge-case detail slips under live pressure even when the core concept is solid.
- **Known prep method, not executed:** the CSE370 Project Viva is a different shape from the general "insufficient preparation" entries — the correct prep method (trace each feature end-to-end) wasn't undiscovered, it had already been given directly by faculty at an earlier consultation, and still wasn't run before the viva. The gap is execution of an already-identified plan, not absence of one. Same session also produced a stack-choice lesson: the team saw the faculty's own reference project and didn't register it as a signal about grading/tracing ease — worth treating tech-stack choice as something to consult the faculty on, not decide on technical merit alone.
- **Deliberate health-protective task deprioritization (positive signal):** two quizzes were skipped by conscious choice while still physically capable of attempting them — a deliberate decision to preserve recovery capacity ahead of a higher-stakes, non-droppable exam rather than push through on two droppable, low-stakes quizzes. Proactive triage under illness: check which at-risk tasks are actually droppable before spending scarce capacity on them. **Extended to a non-droppable component:** a missed CSE370 assignment broke from this pattern in one respect — it was not droppable and already unsalvageable under a hard "no late submission" rule. The choice here was to accept a known, bounded loss rather than spend scarce recovery-period time chasing an appeal judged unlikely to succeed.
- **Midterm cycle confirms the practice-gap pattern at the highest stakes yet:** all three Midterms restate the "Theory without application" and "Skipping practice materials" patterns above, but at the semester's highest-weight assessments instead of quizzes. Severity graded roughly with how much illness had displaced prep time in the run-up to each. Direct trigger for two corrective decisions: (1) a differentiated pre-filled-note-and-print strategy for the two front-loaded courses versus full live note-taking for the third, aimed at closing coverage gaps earlier; (2) a round-robin daily task breakdown for any future deadline-bound task, aimed at the late-start pattern documented throughout this file.
- **Per-layer ownership hides individual contribution from faculty:** a CSE370 project checkpoint was solo-built under a frontend-infra/backend-infra/game-mechanics team split — one member wrote 100% of the checkpoint's code, but faculty's grading lens only registers backend/database work as gradeable, so the smaller "infra" half that member actually owned looked, from faculty's seat, like the teammate nominally assigned to that layer had done everything. Already corrected structurally: every member now owns one fully-owned feature (frontend + backend + SQL) per build cycle, which removes this failure mode by construction.
- **A milder variant of "theory without application" — moderate prep, format-specific gaps:** CSE321 Quiz 3 reads differently from earlier entries — prep was self-rated as moderate, and results didn't split along a clean calculation-vs-conceptual line: a well-drilled forward-direction calculation and a scenario/decide question both went well; a reverse-direction calculation (a format never tested before) and a step-by-step procedural-writing question did not. Not a single content gap, but two specific under-drilled formats. **Recurred in a different course, same shape:** CSE370 Quiz 4 — theory was solid enough to reconstruct the entire answer chain correctly once talked through post-quiz, but the specific under-drilled format is live scenario-to-named-problem recognition under time pressure — spotting which named concept a described scenario matches, in the moment, rather than being told the category first.
- **Late-start pattern reaches a lab assessment with no fundamentals fallback, worst outcome yet:** CSE321 Lab Assessment 2 is the "Late start" pattern recurring in its most severe form — the confirmed date was known 6 days ahead, but review only started 1 hour before the sharp start time, leaving no time to work through the material cold. Contrast directly with Lab Assessment 1: that assessment also followed a late start, but scored acceptably because retained HSC-era C fundamentals covered the gap. The newer material had no equivalent fallback — so this time the same late-start pattern produced a much thinner result.
- **Pure exam-technique failure — a deferred question never marked, then forgotten:** the CSE331 Final lost its single largest chunk of marks not to a knowledge gap but to a procedural slip: the plan going in was to skip a question and return to it after the rest, since it was a known weak area — but the question was never physically flagged as "come back to" on the paper, and was then simply never returned to. Concrete fix for future sittings: any question deliberately deferred gets a visible mark on the paper the instant it's deferred, and the last few minutes of any exam are spent scanning specifically for marked-but-unanswered questions, not just reviewing already-attempted ones.
- **Grading-margin miscalibration for A/A-:** stated directly after seeing the CSE331 result — the actual margin needed for an A/A- band was underestimated going into the semester. Large exams (Midterm, Final) routinely cost 3–4 marks even in reasonably-prepared subjects just from the mechanics of a big paper under time pressure — meaning an A/A- outcome requires near-perfect execution on every *other* segment (quizzes, assignments, labs) to absorb that unavoidable bleed, not just "doing well overall." Going forward, the smaller/lower-stakes components need to be budgeted as the actual margin of safety for a high grade band, precisely because they're the components where near-perfect is realistically achievable.
- **Course difficulty curve misjudged — deceptively easy start:** one course was underestimated for the whole semester, not just at the Midterm — it was genuinely easy through its early lectures, and that early ease, combined with one course actively struggling and another going well, meant it didn't get proportionate attention until it was already behind. Contrast directly with the course that ran the opposite curve: heavy material from Week 1, a large chunk of marks lost by Midterm before any of it was recognized as a front-loaded-difficulty course rather than a slow-ramp one. Actionable for future semesters: assess each course's actual difficulty curve in the first 1–2 weeks rather than assuming a flat or slow ramp, and allocate early-semester attention by that assessment, not by which course currently feels hardest in the moment.
- **Insufficient hands-on coding volume, masked by lab-faculty leniency:** ties together the lab quiz/lab assessment entries under a single explicit acknowledgment — total hands-on coding practice this semester was not nearly enough, and the graded outcomes are the direct evidence for it. What kept the semester from being worse was lab faculty grading leniency, which shouldn't be counted on as a standing safety net going forward.
