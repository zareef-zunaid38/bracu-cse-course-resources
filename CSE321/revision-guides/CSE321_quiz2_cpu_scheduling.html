📖 Theory ▼
CPU–I/O Burst Cycle & Process TypesLOW

Processes alternate between a CPU burst (computing) and an I/O burst (waiting on I/O), repeating until a final CPU burst ends in termination.

  • CPU-bound: few, long CPU bursts (e.g. scientific computing)
  • I/O-bound: many, short CPU bursts (e.g. interactive programs, databases)
Scheduling Criteria & Core FormulasHIGH

Five criteria: CPU utilization (max), throughput (max), turnaround time (min), waiting time (min), response time (min).

Why it matters: Every analytical question reduces to computing these three: TAT = CT − AT, WT = TAT − BT, RT = first CPU time − AT. Get the formula order wrong and every downstream number is wrong.
When Does Scheduling Occur? Preemptive vs Non-preemptiveMED

Four trigger points: (1) running→waiting, (2) running→ready, (3) waiting→ready, (4) terminates.

  • Scheduling only at (1) and (4) → non-preemptive
  • Scheduling at all four → preemptive
FCFS — First Come First ServedMED

Serves processes strictly in arrival order. Non-preemptive.

Convoy effect: one long process at the front makes every short process behind it wait excessively — average waiting time balloons even though nothing is "wrong" with the algorithm's logic.
SJF vs SRTFHIGH

Non-preemptive SJF: once started, a process runs to completion; the shortest burst is only picked at scheduling decision points.

SRTF (preemptive SJF): on every new arrival, compare its burst to the current process's remaining time — preempt if shorter.

Common error: treating SJF and SRTF as interchangeable. SJF never interrupts a running process; SRTF re-checks on every arrival. SRTF is provably optimal for average WT, but risks starving long jobs.
Priority Scheduling & AgingHIGH

CPU goes to the highest-priority process. Can be preemptive or non-preemptive. Convention (lower number = higher priority, or the reverse) varies — always check what the question states.

Starvation: low-priority processes may never run if higher-priority ones keep arriving. Fix — Aging: gradually increase the priority of processes the longer they wait.
Round Robin (RR)HIGH

Preemptive. Each process gets a fixed time quantum q; if unfinished, it goes to the back of a circular ready queue.

  • q very large → degenerates to FCFS
  • q very small → high responsiveness, but excessive context-switch overhead
  • Rule of thumb: q large enough that ~80% of CPU bursts finish within one quantum
Response Time ≠ Waiting Time here: RT is measured only at the FIRST CPU allocation. A process can wait again after that first slice, but RT is already fixed. This distinction does not exist for non-preemptive algorithms (there, RT = WT).
MLQ vs MLFQ — and the 5 MLFQ RulesHIGH

MLQ: multiple queues, each with its own algorithm; processes are permanently assigned to one queue by type. Risk: starvation of low-priority queues without time slicing.

MLFQ: processes can move between queues based on observed behavior — CPU-heavy jobs get demoted, short/I-O-bound jobs stay high.

Rule 1Priority(A) > Priority(B) → A runs
Rule 2Priority(A) = Priority(B) → Round Robin between them
Rule 3New job enters at the highest priority queue
Rule 4Using up the full time allotment at a level → demoted (regardless of how many times it yielded)
Rule 5After period S, boost ALL jobs back to the top queue (prevents starvation of demoted CPU-bound jobs)
❓ Short Questions ▼

Tap a question to reveal the answer. Try to recall first — this mirrors Quiz 1's short written-answer format.

What is the convoy effect, and which algorithm suffers from it? HIGH
FCFS. A long process at the head of the queue forces every shorter process behind it to wait far longer than its own burst time would suggest, driving average waiting time up even though each process is served fairly in arrival order.
Why is SRTF considered optimal, and what is its main drawback? HIGH
SRTF minimizes average waiting time because it always runs whichever process will finish soonest, including preempting mid-execution when a shorter job arrives. Drawback: it requires knowing burst times in advance (usually estimated, not known), and long processes can starve if short ones keep arriving.
What is starvation in priority scheduling, and how does aging fix it? HIGH
Low-priority processes may never get the CPU if higher-priority processes keep arriving. Aging gradually raises the priority of a process the longer it waits, guaranteeing it eventually becomes the highest priority and runs.
How does the Round Robin time quantum affect performance at each extreme? MED
Very large q → RR degenerates into FCFS (most processes finish inside one quantum). Very small q → high responsiveness but excessive context-switch overhead dominates useful work. Rule of thumb: pick q so roughly 80% of CPU bursts complete within one quantum.
Differentiate Multilevel Queue (MLQ) from Multilevel Feedback Queue (MLFQ). HIGH
MLQ assigns each process permanently to one queue by type, with fixed priority or time-sliced scheduling between queues. MLFQ lets processes move between queues based on observed behavior — CPU-bound jobs get demoted, I/O-bound/short jobs stay near the top — which is more adaptive but more complex and starvation-prone without a priority boost.
Why is Priority Boost (MLFQ Rule 5) needed? MED
Without it, a CPU-bound job that gets demoted stays permanently at the bottom queue and can starve if higher queues never empty. Periodically boosting every job back to the top queue guarantees it eventually gets scheduled again.
Is Response Time equal to Waiting Time in Round Robin? Why or why not? HIGH
No. Response Time is fixed at the moment of the FIRST CPU allocation only. A process can be preempted and wait again afterward, which keeps adding to its total Waiting Time — but that later waiting doesn't change RT. For non-preemptive algorithms, RT = WT because there's only ever one CPU allocation.
Which of the four scheduling trigger points make an algorithm preemptive vs non-preemptive? LOW
Scheduling only at "running→waiting" and "terminates" → non-preemptive (cooperative). Scheduling at all four points, including "running→ready" (timer interrupt) and "waiting→ready" (I/O done), → preemptive.
🧮 Analytical Questions ▼

Full worked Gantt-chart traces, one per algorithm. This is the highest-value question type based on Quiz 1's format.

Q1 FCFS — P1(AT0,BT24), P2(AT1,BT3), P3(AT2,BT3). Draw the Gantt chart and compute avg TAT/WT. MED ▼
1
Order strictly by arrival time: P1, P2, P3.
2
Run each to completion in that order: P1 finishes at 24, P2 at 27, P3 at 30.
3
CT − AT = TAT; TAT − BT = WT for each process, then average.
Gantt: [P1─────0..24][P2─24..27][P3─27..30] Process | AT | BT | CT | TAT | WT P1 | 0 | 24 | 24 | 24 | 0 P2 | 1 | 3 | 27 | 26 | 23 P3 | 2 | 3 | 30 | 28 | 25 Avg TAT = 26, Avg WT = 16
Key insight: P1's long burst is why P2/P3's waiting times are so disproportionate to their own burst — this is the convoy effect made concrete.
Q2 Non-preemptive SJF — P1(0,7), P2(2,4), P3(4,1), P4(5,4). Draw the Gantt chart and compute avg TAT/WT. HIGH ▼
1
At t=0, only P1 has arrived → must run P1 (no choice yet). Runs to completion at t=7.
2
At t=7, P2(4), P3(1), P4(4) are all in the queue → pick shortest burst: P3.
3
At t=8, P2(4) and P4(4) tie → break the tie with FCFS (P2 arrived first) → run P2, then P4.
Gantt: [P1─0..7][P3─7..8][P2─8..12][P4─12..16] Process | AT | BT | CT | TAT | WT P1 | 0 | 7 | 7 | 7 | 0 P2 | 2 | 4 | 12 | 10 | 6 P3 | 4 | 1 | 8 | 4 | 3 P4 | 5 | 4 | 16 | 11 | 7 Avg TAT = 8, Avg WT = 4
Exam note: the tie-break rule (FCFS among equal bursts) is an easy point to lose if you pick arbitrarily instead of by arrival order — state the tie-break explicitly in your answer.
Q3 SRTF (preemptive SJF) — same processes as Q2: P1(0,7), P2(2,4), P3(4,1), P4(5,4). Trace it. HIGH ▼
1
t=0: P1 starts, 7 remaining.
2
t=2: P2 arrives (BT=4). P1 has 5 remaining. 4 < 5 → preempt P1, run P2.
3
t=4: P3 arrives (BT=1). P2 has 2 remaining. 1 < 2 → preempt P2, run P3.
4
t=5: P3 finishes; P4 arrives (BT=4) at the same instant. Compare all remaining: P1=5, P2=2, P4=4 → pick P2 (still shortest remaining).
5
t=7: P2 finishes. Compare P1=5, P4=4 → pick P4. t=11: P4 finishes → run P1 to completion at t=16.
Gantt: [P1─0..2][P2─2..4][P3─4..5][P2─5..7][P4─7..11][P1─11..16] Process | AT | BT | CT | TAT | WT P1 | 0 | 7 | 16 | 16 | 9 P2 | 2 | 4 | 7 | 5 | 1 P3 | 4 | 1 | 5 | 1 | 0 P4 | 5 | 4 | 11 | 6 | 2 Avg TAT = 7, Avg WT = 3
Common error: forgetting to re-evaluate at every new arrival, including comparing against the process that just finished at the same timestamp (t=5 here has two events at once — a P3 completion and a P4 arrival). Missing that double-event is the single most likely place to lose marks on this question type.
Key insight: notice P1 — the process with the longest burst — ends up running last despite starting first. This is exactly the starvation risk SRTF carries.
Q4 Non-preemptive Priority — P1(BT10,Pr3), P2(BT1,Pr1), P3(BT2,Pr4), P4(BT1,Pr5), P5(BT5,Pr2), all arrive at t=0, lower number = higher priority. MED ▼
1
All arrive together, so just sort by priority number ascending: P2(1) → P5(2) → P1(3) → P3(4) → P4(5).
2
Run each to completion in that order and accumulate completion times.
Gantt: [P2─0..1][P5─1..6][P1─6..16][P3─16..18][P4─18..19] Process | BT | CT | TAT | WT P2 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 0 P5 | 5 | 6 | 6 | 1 P1 | 10 | 16 | 16 | 6 P3 | 2 | 18 | 18 | 16 P4 | 1 | 19 | 19 | 18 Avg TAT = 12, Avg WT = 8.2
Exam note: always check the question's priority convention first — "lower number = higher priority" and "higher number = higher priority" are both used, and this table's order flips completely if you assume the wrong one.
Q5 Round Robin (q=4) — P1(0,24), P2(0,3), P3(0,3). Trace it and compute RT as well as TAT/WT. HIGH ▼
1
Queue order at t=0: P1, P2, P3 (all arrived together, FCFS order into the circular queue).
2
P1 runs its full quantum (4ms), goes to back of queue with 20 remaining. P2 runs 3ms and finishes (burst < q). P3 runs 3ms and finishes.
3
Only P1 remains — it now just runs its remaining 20ms in 4ms chunks with no one else to rotate with, finishing at t=30.
Gantt: [P1─0..4][P2─4..7][P3─7..10][P1─10..14][P1─14..18][P1─18..22][P1─22..26][P1─26..30] Process | AT | BT | CT | TAT | WT | RT P1 | 0 | 24 | 30 | 30 | 6 | 0 P2 | 0 | 3 | 7 | 7 | 4 | 4 P3 | 0 | 3 | 10 | 10 | 7 | 7 Avg TAT=15.67, Avg WT=5.67, Avg RT=3.67
Exam note: RT is the FIRST CPU slice only — P1 gets CPU at t=0 so RT=0 even though it doesn't finish until t=30. This is the exact RT ≠ WT distinction flagged in the Theory section; expect it to be tested directly.
Q6 MLFQ walkthrough — a new job enters a 3-queue MLFQ (Q0: q=8, Q1: q=16, Q2: FCFS). Trace where it ends up if it needs 30ms of CPU total. MED ▼
1
Job enters at Q0 (Rule 3). Runs 8ms, uses the full quantum without finishing (30ms total needed) → demoted to Q1 (Rule 4).
2
In Q1, runs up to 16ms. 8 + 16 = 24ms consumed, 6ms remaining — still uses the full quantum → demoted to Q2.
3
Q2 is FCFS (no quantum limit) → job runs its remaining 6ms to completion, unless a Priority Boost (Rule 5) happens first and resets it to Q0.
Key insight: this is exactly the mechanism that separates short jobs (finish inside Q0's 8ms, never demoted) from long CPU-bound jobs (progressively demoted, but still guaranteed to run eventually because of the boost).
∑ Mathematics ▼
Core Metrics
TAT = CT − AT WT = TAT − BT RT = First CPU time − AT
CT = Completion Time, AT = Arrival Time, BT = Burst Time. Compute TAT before WT — WT depends on it.
SJF/SRTF Burst-Time Prediction (Exponential Average)
τ(n+1) = α·t(n) + (1−α)·τ(n)
τ(n+1) = next predicted burst, t(n) = actual n-th burst, τ(n) = previous prediction, α = weight (typically 0.5). Used because true burst length isn't known in advance — SJF/SRTF must estimate it.
Ex Worked example: previous prediction τ(n)=10, actual burst t(n)=6, α=0.5. Find τ(n+1). HIGH ▼
1
Plug into the formula: τ(n+1) = 0.5×6 + 0.5×10.
2
= 3 + 5 = 8. The next prediction moves halfway toward the most recent actual burst.
Pattern: with α=0.5, each new prediction is always the simple average of the previous prediction and the latest actual value — the fastest way to sanity-check your arithmetic on this formula.

🧠 Mnemonics & Memory Aids ▼
TAT → WT → RT, in that order
"Complete, then Wait, then Respond" — compute TAT first (CT−AT), then WT depends on it (TAT−BT). RT is separate: First CPU − AT. Never compute WT before TAT.
SRTF = "Shortest Remaining, Always"
Reminder that SRTF re-checks the shortest REMAINING time on every single new arrival — not just at the start, unlike non-preemptive SJF.
MLFQ Rules: "Enter High, Earn Your Spot, Get Bumped, Get Boosted"
Enter High = Rule 3 (new job → top queue). Earn Your Spot = Rules 1&2 (priority order, RR on ties). Get Bumped = Rule 4 (demote after using full quantum). Get Boosted = Rule 5 (periodic reset to top, prevents starvation).
RR's 80/20
Quantum should be sized so ~80% of bursts finish inside one quantum. Too big → FCFS. Too small → overhead eats the CPU.
